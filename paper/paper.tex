\begin{abstract}
Prompt-injection detectors can reject benign text that contains attack-like
keywords. We describe Injection-Aware MPNet, a fine-tuned sentence encoder
whose contrastive training samples injection--benign-trigger pairs twice as
often as each other pair type, followed by an XGBoost classifier. An audit
of the repository found that the historical encoder training pools overlap
with benchmark inputs and with the encoder validation pool. The available
results therefore do not establish performance on unseen inputs or a causal
reduction in over-defense. We retain only explicitly labelled historical
counts, distinguish the local synthetic trigger benchmark from official
NotInject, and report a new, bounded single-seed re-evaluation on 2,315
held-out inputs. Doubling injection--benign-trigger pairs raises attack
recall from 48.30\% to 64.20\% relative to uniform pairing, but also raises
the false-positive rate from 25.50\% to 36.78\%. This experiment does not
support a general reduction in over-defense. A separate supervised encoder
pilot on 447 fresh BrowseSafe pages improves recall and false-positive rates
for two training seeds, while a third seed degrades AUC and exceeds its
validation false-positive budget on the holdout. A six-epoch follow-up with
a disclosed mixed-precision repair improves recall for all three seeds on
a separate 453-page holdout, reaching 66.36--69.12\% recall at empirical
FPR 2.54--4.66\%. False positives increase for two seeds, and population
FPR below 5\% remains unestablished. Previously reported latency
distributions, embedding-distance improvements, and comparative superiority
remain withdrawn; fresh timings are reported with a limited measurement scope.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) deployed as conversational assistants and as
components of agentic systems are exposed to a steady stream of untrusted
text---direct user input, retrieved web pages, emails, and messages from
other agents. Prompt injection attacks exploit this exposure by smuggling
adversarial instructions into the input stream. While the attack surface and
threat models in agentic deployments are still being mapped---e.g.\
\citet{lee2024prompt} on cross-agent ``prompt infection''---a robust input
filter remains a foundational primitive for any defence stack.

Current input-side detectors face two recurring limitations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Over-defense.} Detectors that lean on keyword matching or
          aggressive classifiers reject benign prompts that merely contain
          attack-adjacent terms (``system'', ``ignore'', ``bypass''),
          breaking legitimate use cases. \citet{li2024injecguard} showed
          that guardrail accuracy can fall to around 60\%
          on the NotInject over-defense benchmark.
    \item \textbf{Lexical shortcuts.} Models trained on imbalanced corpora
          (where most malicious prompts contain trigger words and most
          benign prompts do not) may learn a trigger-word/malicious
          correlation rather than the intended distinction.
\end{itemize}

We investigate a focused detection approach:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injection-Aware MPNet.} Cosine-similarity training of
          \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} with additional injection--benign-trigger
          pairs, intended to discourage reliance on shared keywords.
    \item \textbf{Embedding classification.} A 768-dimensional embedding
          feeds XGBoost; the historical operating threshold is $0.764$.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation audit.} We trace the available numerical
          claims to source code and saved counts, and identify leakage,
          missing provenance, and measurements that require replacement.
\end{itemize}
The intended benefit of the pair weighting is a hypothesis. A controlled
ablation and independent evaluation are needed to establish it. Coordination
and response modules exist in the repository, but this paper makes no
empirical claim about their protection of downstream agents.

\section{Background \& Related Work}

\textbf{Prompt Injection:} Early work identified direct prompt injection \citep{perez2022ignore} and indirect injection via retrieved context \citep{greshake2023more}. In multi-agent systems, ``prompt infection'' \citep{lee2024prompt} describes how malicious prompts can replicate across agents.

\textbf{Defense Mechanisms:} Current defenses span multiple paradigms with distinct trade-offs:

\subsection{Training-Time Defenses}
\textit{StruQ} \citep{chen2024struq} separates prompts and data through a
secure front-end and trains the LLM to follow instructions in the prompt
channel. \textit{SecAlign} \citep{chen2024secalign} uses preference
optimization on secure and insecure outputs for injected inputs. Both
modify the protected model through training; our approach instead trains
an external detector. Their downstream attack-success metrics are not
directly comparable to text-classification accuracy.

\subsection{LLM-Based Defenses}
\textit{PromptArmor} \citep{si2025promptarmor} prompts an off-the-shelf LLM
to detect and remove injected content before an agent processes it. Its
paper reports false-positive and false-negative rates below 1\% on
AgentDojo with several guardrail models. We have not reproduced that
experiment and make no comparative latency or superiority claim.

\subsection{Over-Defense Mitigation}
\textit{InjecGuard} \citep{li2024injecguard} introduces NotInject, a
339-sample benign benchmark containing trigger words, and the Mitigating
Over-defense for Free (MOF) training strategy. Our local synthetic trigger
set is distinct from that benchmark. Both approaches target trigger-word
bias, but matched data, model capacity, and evaluation conditions are needed
to compare their training strategies.

\section{Threat Model}

\textbf{Adversary capability.} The adversary controls at least one
untrusted text channel into the LLM application: a chat input, a fetched
web page, an email body, a tool-call response, or a message from a
peer agent in a multi-agent setup. The adversary's text is concatenated
with system prompts and trusted context before reaching the LLM.

\textbf{Adversary goals.} (i) \emph{Policy bypass}: cause the LLM to
violate its system-level instructions. (ii) \emph{Data exfiltration}:
extract sensitive context (system prompts, conversation history, tool
outputs) into the response. (iii) \emph{Lateral movement (multi-agent
setting)}: have one agent issue commands that another agent then executes.

\textbf{Defender capability.} The defender places our detector inline
between the untrusted text channel and the downstream LLM. The detector
sees raw text, returns a binary decision plus a confidence score, and may
strip or quarantine flagged inputs.

\textbf{Out of scope.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Adversarial attacks on the LLM's parameters} (weight
          extraction, model inversion, supply-chain attacks on the base
          model).
    \item \emph{Adaptive attacks on the detector itself} (gradient-based
          attacks such as GCG/AutoDAN). No model-matched adaptive evaluation is established here; see
          Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
    \item \emph{Multi-turn attacks that build context across many
          messages.} Our detector evaluates each message independently.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.} The primary focus of this paper is \textbf{single-turn,
text-based prompt injection}. We additionally audit historical results for text extracted
from HTML-embedded attacks (Section~\ref{sec:browsesafe}).

\section{Detection System Design}
\label{sec:design}

The embedding classifier receives a text input, encodes it with MPNet,
and applies XGBoost to the embedding. The returned injection score is
thresholded to obtain a binary decision; calibration as a real-world
probability has not been established. Historical records identify an
embedding-only detector. The repository also contains regex matching and
behavioural monitoring modules, but no matched experiment establishes their
incremental accuracy or false-positive cost.

\paragraph{Multi-agent coordination and response.}
The repository contains guard, messaging, circuit-breaker, and quarantine
modules. These are implementation components, not evidence of resistance
to prompt propagation or downstream compromise. In particular,
\path{OVONMessage.verify_integrity} accepts a claimed verification flag
in message metadata rather than verifying an authenticated signature. Authentication and
multi-agent effectiveness must be independently implemented and tested
before a security claim is made. PeerGuard \citep{fan2025peerguard}
is related work on mutual reasoning against backdoors, not validation of
these modules.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Decision rule}
For embedding $\mathbf{e}(x)$, let $s(x)$ be the XGBoost injection score.
The historical classifier uses
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(x) = \mathbf{1}[s(x) \ge 0.764].
\end{equation}
The classifier training entry point hard-codes this threshold. The saved
metadata records it, but does not establish selection on a held-out
validation set. We therefore do not describe it as optimal. A new
operating threshold must be selected on validation data excluded from both
training stages, then frozen before testing.

\subsection{Injection-Aware MPNet contrastive fine-tuning}

The historical encoder uses \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} and requests the
following data. Loader calls are recorded in
\path{training/finetune_mpnet_embeddings.py}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injection text from SaTML (limit 1{,}500), deepset (up to 500
          injections), and LLMail (limit 500).
    \item BrowseSafe (limit 2{,}000), whose extracted text is assigned to
          injection or safe pools according to its label.
    \item Safe deepset text (up to 1{,}000 safe samples).
    \item Official NotInject (limit 500) and local synthetic trigger text
          (limit 1{,}000), both treated as benign.
\end{itemize}
These are loader limits, not observed corpus sizes: the local synthetic
loader generates only 247 prompts, and deepset's limit applies separately
to each class. The original pipeline does not exclude benchmark text from
these pools. Its validation pairs also draw from the last 200 entries of
pools used for training. These overlaps invalidate an interpretation of
its validation score as performance on unseen text.

\paragraph{Pair construction.} From the loaded text, the script samples
14{,}000 \texttt{InputExample} pairs as in Table~\ref{tab:pairs}. The
defining methodological choice is \emph{doubling} the injection-vs.\
benign-with-trigger dissimilar pairs (4{,}000 vs.\ 2{,}000 of every other
pair type), intended to emphasize prompts that share trigger words but differ in intent.
This changes sampling frequency rather than assigning an explicit per-pair
loss coefficient.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Contrastive pair counts and target cosine similarity labels.}
\label{tab:pairs}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Pair type} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Target similarity} \\
\midrule
Injection--Injection                       & 2{,}000 & 1.0 \\
Safe--Safe                                 & 2{,}000 & 1.0 \\
Benign-trigger--Benign-trigger             & 2{,}000 & 1.0 \\
Injection--Safe                            & 2{,}000 & 0.0 \\
\textbf{Injection--Benign-trigger}         & \textbf{4{,}000} & \textbf{0.0} \\
Safe--Benign-trigger                       & 2{,}000 & 0.8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 0.8 target for safe vs.\ benign-with-trigger acknowledges that both
are benign but stylistically distinct, encoding a desired similarity rather than a measured geometric outcome.

The encoder is trained with cosine-similarity loss
(\path{losses.CosineSimilarityLoss}, MSE):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl(s_i - \cos(\mathbf{e}_1^{(i)}, \mathbf{e}_2^{(i)})\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2$ are 768-dim outputs of the fine-tuned
encoder and $s_i$ is the target from Table~\ref{tab:pairs}.

\paragraph{Recorded training configuration.}
The model card records batch size 4, learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$,
100 warm-up steps, and a four-epoch configuration. The encoder script does
not explicitly pass the learning rate, so reproducing the model-card
configuration requires setting it rather than relying on a library default.
The last model-card log entry reports Spearman correlation 0.9040 at step
12{,}500 (epoch 3.5714); this is not proof of a plateau-triggered early stop.
The validation-pool overlap described above limits the interpretation of
that correlation.

\subsection{XGBoost classifier on the fine-tuned embeddings}

The 768-dim embeddings feed an XGBoost classifier
(\texttt{n\_estimators=300}, \texttt{max\_depth=6},
\texttt{learning\_rate=0.05}, \texttt{subsample=0.8},
\path{colsample_bytree=0.8}, \texttt{tree\_method=hist}). The classifier
was trained on the corpus recorded in
\path{models/injection_aware_mpnet_metadata.json}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Safe benign: 2{,}667 samples;
    \item Benign-with-trigger: 570 samples;
    \item Malicious: 413 rows of templated injections and a deepset slice.
\end{itemize}

The metadata counts rows, not necessarily unique prompts: the safe-text
and malicious-text generators repeat templates. The classifier entry point
calls \path{EmbeddingClassifier.train}, which fits all supplied embeddings
without a validation split. Its configured parameters do not include
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}. Thus neither a held-out classifier validation
set nor class-ratio loss weighting is established by this training path.
The separate \texttt{train\_on\_dataset} method offers a 90/10 split; its
existence does not establish that the recorded classifier used that path.

\subsection{Metrics and uncertainty}
We recompute accuracy, recall, and false-positive rate (FPR) from counts:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},\quad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad
\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}.
\end{equation}
A metric with zero denominator is N/A, not zero. Table~\ref{tab:per-dataset-metrics}
includes nominal 95\% Wilson intervals for these proportions. The intervals
summarize finite-count uncertainty under an independent Bernoulli assumption;
they do not correct training overlap, duplicate prompts, or selection bias.
No paired model comparison or McNemar significance claim is made.

We withdraw the prior state-of-the-art and lower-latency claims. The

They are excluded from this draft. A comparison between doubled and uniform
pair sampling, using the same unique texts and held-out predictions, is
needed to test whether pair weighting improves over-defense performance.

\subsection{Latency evidence}
\label{sec:latency}
The earlier CDF and boxplot are excluded because
\path{paper/generate_latency_charts.py} generates lognormal random samples
rather than loading measured latencies. The benchmark runner times whole
prediction batches and assigns batch duration divided by batch size to
each item. Quantiles of these amortized times do not measure single-request
tail latency. In the embedding-classifier path, this timer includes
\texttt{predict\_proba}, which itself generates embeddings; source inspection
does not support a blanket claim that it times only XGBoost.

A replacement measurement must identify hardware, model hashes, input
lengths, warm-up, batch size, synchronization, and whether preprocessing
is included. Neither the old GPU headline nor a new CPU latency claim is
established here.

\subsection{BrowseSafe: text extraction, not cross-modality validation}
\label{sec:browsesafe}
BrowseSafe \citep{perplexity2025browsesafe} provides HTML-embedded prompt
injections. Our loader removes script and style elements and extracts text,
with a raw-prefix fallback for short extracted content. It does not evaluate
a rendered browser, CSS visibility, screenshots, or downstream agent actions.
The historical training pool also includes BrowseSafe text. Consequently,
the old result cannot establish unseen-domain or cross-modality transfer.
The text summary reports 98.0\% accuracy and 94.1\% recall, while the old
manuscript table reported 98.2\% and 96.7\%; neither is promoted to a new
performance claim without matched sample counts and prediction provenance.

\section{Bounded Colab Re-evaluation}
\label{sec:colab}
A new experiment completed on September 11, 2026, using a Tesla T4 in
Google Colab. It starts from the public base MPNet encoder rather than the
historical checkpoint. Pinned source revisions and capped source prefixes
produce 6,370 nonempty rows; one empty LLMail body is recorded as rejected.
After removing 1,024 duplicate texts or truncated model inputs and three
conflicting-label rows, the manifest contains 2,286 training, 742 validation,
and 2,315 test inputs. Normalized text and 384-token input hashes, known
LLMail team groups, and synthetic template families do not cross splits.
Both training stages use the same training membership. All 339 official
NotInject and 247 historical local synthetic prompts are test-only.
Unknown source provenance and semantic near-duplicates remain uncontrolled.

The comparison uses base MPNet plus XGBoost, a uniformly sampled pair
mixture, and the doubled injection--trigger mixture. Both fine-tuned
variants use 14,000 pairs per epoch, four epochs, batch size four, learning
rate $5\times10^{-5}$, 100 warmup steps, and seed 42. The classifier threshold
maximizes validation balanced accuracy, with the highest threshold breaking
ties, and is saved before test inference. The resulting thresholds are
0.880826, 0.772123, and 0.999148, respectively. Test labels do not select them.

\subsection{Validation diagnosis and a controlled coverage correction}
Inspection of the frozen validation split identified input loss before
classification. Of 410 BrowseSafe validation pages, 363 exceed the
382-content-token budget (384 tokens including special tokens). A fixed
lexical locator finds instruction phrases only beyond the first window in
52 attack-labelled pages; all three variants miss 39 of these. Manual
inspection confirms explicit instructions outside the window in selected
cases. In another 21 attack-labelled pages, a phrase matches raw HTML but
not extracted text; inspected examples lose payloads in hidden input
values, comments, or data attributes. These counts are locator-assisted,
not exhaustive attack-span annotations. Quoted instructional discussion
also occurs in non-injection pages.

Label and sampling limitations remain. All 136 training trigger examples
come from three synthetic template families, while the 68 validation
trigger examples share one education family; some substitutions are
ungrammatical. No LLMail group reaches validation. A source-only score
using training attack prevalence attains pooled validation ROC-AUC 0.779,
showing that source recognition can inflate pooled evidence. This does
not establish what features any encoder actually learned. A character
TF-IDF diagnostic flags 97 validation inputs at cosine similarity at least
0.90 to training text, including seven nearest pairs with opposite labels;
similar pages with different inserted payloads need not be mislabeled.
Non-injection labels also do not imply general harmlessness, and some
isolated competition messages lack the context needed to judge their label.
No labels were automatically changed.

A one-factor follow-up retains frozen base MPNet, original training heads,
labels, HTML extraction, and classifier parameters. The correction scores
overlapping 382-token windows with 96-token overlap and takes the maximum
document score. Seeds 17, 42, and 73 vary XGBoost subsampling only; this is
not a multi-seed encoder-training experiment. Each method's threshold is
selected on validation balanced accuracy and frozen before final inference.
The fixed, previously unused BrowseSafe test interval 600--1599 yields
919 inputs (445 attacks, 474 non-injections) after excluding 81 exact or
highly similar inputs against prior raw data and within the fresh holdout.
The similarity cutoff is 0.90; unknown website and semantic dependence
remain. No seed or operating point is selected using the final scores.

\subsection{Supervised encoder pilot across training seeds}
\label{sec:supervised-pilot}
A subsequent controlled experiment compares a frozen MPNet encoder with
full encoder fine-tuning through the same linear binary-classification head.
Both arms preserve unchanged raw HTML, including comments, attributes,
and scripts, and score all overlapping 382-content-token windows. The
maximum window logit gives one page-level binary cross-entropy loss;
page labels are not assigned to individual windows. The fine-tuned arm
selects the maximum without gradients and recomputes that window with
gradients. Dropout is disabled in both arms, making this a max-loss
subgradient apart from finite-precision batching differences.

The pilot uses 256 training pages (128 per class), 192 validation pages
(96 per class), and 447 previously unscored holdout pages (223 attacks,
224 non-injections), all from the same pinned BrowseSafe revision.
Training and validation retain their prior partition membership. A fixed
hash order samples eligible pages after character TF-IDF overlap rejection
at cosine similarity 0.90. The fresh official-test interval 1600--2099
excludes 53 candidates against prior data or other new holdout pages.
There are 18,409 training, 14,823 validation, and 34,189 holdout windows.
A bounded label review quarantines one ambiguous training page without
relabeling it; other source labels remain machine-generated supervision.
Single-source sampling removes across-source label imbalance from this
comparison but cannot remove unknown website or template dependence.

Seeds 17, 42, and 73 vary head initialization and training order, with
matched initialization and order between arms. Unlike the earlier window
follow-up, these include three actual encoder-training runs. Each arm
receives at most two epochs, four pages per optimizer step, AdamW weight
decay 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1. Encoder and head learning rates are
$10^{-5}$ and $10^{-3}$. Execution uses an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB with
float16 autocast and float32 parameters. Validation maximizes recall at
empirical FPR at most 5\%, using ROC-AUC and the earliest epoch to break
checkpoint ties. This budget permits at most four validation false
positives; it is not a guarantee on new data. All six checkpoints and
thresholds are frozen before any holdout scoring.

coverage, saved linear-head and historical classifier predictions,
validation selections, and paired training orders passed local verification.
GPU encoder training and inference were not independently repeated.

\subsection{Six-epoch supervised schedule follow-up}
\label{sec:supervised-schedule}
A training/validation-only diagnosis found that seed 42's mean training
loss changed from 0.6946 to 0.6972 over the first two epochs, despite
nonzero encoder updates. Its validation AUC improved in epoch two, but
recall under the FPR budget fell, retaining epoch one. The selected
checkpoint's median top-two-window logit gap was 0.00075, versus
0.0421 and 0.0454 for the other seeds; these checkpoints came from
different epochs. These observations motivate a longer schedule without
establishing a label, pooling or optimizer defect.

The follow-up completes six fixed epochs, retaining the best validation
checkpoint, instead of at most two epochs with early stopping. It keeps
the same 256 training and 192 validation pages, architecture, objective,
raw-HTML window coverage, learning rates, optimizer, precision, seeds,
initialization and page orders. Frozen-encoder controls remain. A separate
453-page holdout contains 217 attacks and 236 non-injections (34,449
windows), from the pinned BrowseSafe official-test interval 2100--2599.
Before scoring, 47 of 500 candidates were excluded by normalized
raw/extracted/first-window matches and character-TF-IDF cosine similarity
at least 0.90 against 7,870 prior inputs or earlier retained candidates.
This lexical screen does not establish website or template independence.
Validation retains the same recall/FPR, AUC and earliest-epoch selection
rule. All twelve prior/new models and thresholds are frozen before
evaluation; no test score is used to select a seed.

\paragraph{Numerical amendment.}
The first attempt stopped at seed 17's update 181: nonfinite-gradient
clipping raised before GradScaler could reduce its scale. No holdout
evaluation had begun. A protocol amendment, frozen before test scoring,
clears invalid gradients, lets GradScaler skip the invalid attempt and
back off, then retries backward on the same retained forward graph.
Persistent nonfinite gradients abort after at most eight retries.
All six trials restart from initialization and complete 384 finite
optimizer updates without omitting a batch. Fine-tuned seeds 17/42/73
retry two batches each, with 2/4/2 invalid attempts; frozen arms have none.
Forced-overflow recovery and persistent-nonfinite rejection checks pass.
All first-two-epoch validation scores, decisions and page orders match
the original runs exactly. This is a schedule comparison with a disclosed
numerical repair, not an unamended one-factor experiment. The overflow
occurs beyond the original two-epoch budget and does not explain the
earlier seed-42 result.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Leakage and remediation status.}
The original encoder loads evaluation-source text before pair construction,
and its validation tail remains in the training pool. The classifier's
separate training entry point also consumes local synthetic trigger text
and deepset injections that are used for evaluation. A split applied only
to classifier embeddings cannot undo prior encoder exposure.

The later source change at commit \texttt{3d1bba8}, merged in
\texttt{1be03c1}, subtracts a benchmark holdout from the encoder pools and
splits internal validation before pair construction. This is a code change,
not evidence of a retrained checkpoint. It also leaves the separate
classifier training entry point unchanged, omits the local synthetic
benchmark from the holdout, and splits row positions without enforcing
unique normalized texts. Its verification script checks a reconstructed
set after subtraction rather than the actual texts used by a trained model.
A model-bound, independent verification remains necessary.

\paragraph{Remaining re-evaluation requirements.}
Section~\ref{sec:colab} provides a new checkpoint-bound experiment with
shared, deduplicated splits and saved predictions for both training stages.
The historical checkpoint and results remain contaminated; the new run does
not retroactively validate them. Broader evidence still requires replication
of the contrastive-pair comparison across encoder-training seeds, stronger
provenance and semantic near-duplicate controls, complete
benchmark coverage, and evaluation of operational or downstream security
outcomes. The original local weight-file deletion has been preserved;
new encoders are stored separately with the downloaded audit evidence.

\paragraph{Scope of security conclusions.}
Single-text classification does not measure downstream attack success,
agent task utility, authentication, quarantine effectiveness, or resistance
to multi-turn and adaptive attacks. The 36-example GCG/AutoDAN-labelled
record identifies an older BIT classifier and counts detector bypasses;
it is not a matched adaptive attack evaluation for Injection-Aware MPNet.
No traceable TensorTrust result for the claimed 83.2\% was located, so that
number is withdrawn. AgentDojo artifacts exist, but a complete evaluation
bound to this MPNet checkpoint is not established by their presence.

\section{Conclusion}
Injection-Aware MPNet implements a testable approach to prompt-injection
detection: emphasize injection--benign-trigger sentence pairs during
encoder training and classify the resulting embeddings. The available
historical evidence does not establish held-out accuracy, a causal reduction
in over-defense, state-of-the-art performance, or production latency.
The new bounded experiment improves recall with doubled pairs at the cost
of substantially more false positives, including on official NotInject.
It does not support a general reduction in over-defense. The revised paper
also records a controlled window-coverage follow-up: small AUC changes across
three classifier seeds accompany substantially more false positives on a
fresh BrowseSafe slice. A supervised raw-HTML encoder pilot improves two
of three training seeds, but the third has worse AUC and exceeds its FPR
budget on a fresh holdout. Extending supervised training to six epochs
with a disclosed numerical repair improves recall across all three seeds
on a separate 453-page holdout, reaching 66.36--69.12\% recall and
2.54--4.66\% empirical FPR. Two seeds incur more false positives;
population FPR below 5\%, broader generalization and the original
contrastive over-defense claim remain unestablished. These experiments
do not support standalone security-gate use. The revised paper
records supported implementation details, historical counts, and the new
checkpoint-bound measurements while making their limitations explicit. Repository materials are available at
\url{https://github.com/goodwiins/prompt-injection-defense}; the new audit
retains checkpoints, manifests, predictions, and a runnable verification
command, while broader security conclusions remain unestablished.
